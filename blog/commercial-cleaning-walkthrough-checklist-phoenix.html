What to Look for in a Commercial Cleaning Walkthrough

Published August 04, 2026 — CleanWorks Solutions

A commercial cleaning walkthrough should be more than a quick lap around the building and a generic price at the end. For Phoenix businesses, the walkthrough is where a cleaning company should learn how your space is used, where dust and soil enter, what areas affect employees or customers most, and what problems your current cleaning routine is missing.

If you are comparing cleaning providers, pay attention to the questions they ask and the details they notice. A good walkthrough should produce a clear scope of work, realistic frequency recommendations, and a quote that matches the actual building. A rushed walkthrough often leads to underbidding, vague promises, missed tasks, and change orders after the contract starts.

The 15-Point Walkthrough Checklist

Use this checklist when a cleaning company visits your office, clinic, school, law firm, retail space, or professional building. You do not need to be a cleaning expert. You just need to know whether the provider is looking closely enough to build a service plan that will actually work.

  1. Entryways and matting. In Phoenix, dust, gravel, landscaping debris, and monsoon mud usually start at the front door. The walkthrough should include mats, thresholds, glass doors, and the first 20 feet of flooring.
  2. High-traffic paths. Hallways, lobby routes, breakroom entrances, and restroom corridors need more attention than quiet private offices.
  3. Floor type and condition. Carpet, tile, polished concrete, luxury vinyl, and VCT all need different maintenance. The provider should identify wear patterns, not just square footage.
  4. Restroom expectations. Ask exactly what is cleaned, disinfected, restocked, and inspected. Restrooms are where inconsistent cleaning becomes obvious fastest.
  5. Breakrooms and kitchens. Counters, sinks, appliance fronts, tables, cabinet handles, trash, odors, and floor edges should all be discussed.
  6. Touch points. Door handles, light switches, railings, shared tables, reception counters, and copier areas should be part of the routine scope.
  7. Dust collection areas. Window sills, chair rails, baseboards, vents, cubicle tops, open shelving, and electronics collect desert dust quickly.
  8. Trash and recycling flow. The provider should understand bin locations, liner sizes, exterior dumpster access, and any locked or restricted areas.
  9. Security and access. Keys, alarm codes, badges, after-hours rules, locked offices, camera areas, and sign-in procedures should be handled clearly.
  10. Medical or confidential areas. Clinics, dental offices, law firms, and financial offices may need HIPAA-aware or confidentiality-aware procedures.
  11. Supply responsibilities. Confirm who provides paper goods, hand soap, liners, disinfectants, and specialty floor products.
  12. Problem areas from the current cleaner. A serious provider will ask what is being missed today and will inspect those areas during the walkthrough.
  13. Frequency by area. Not every room needs the same schedule. Lobbies and restrooms may need more frequent service than storage rooms or low-use offices.
  14. Periodic tasks. Window interiors, vents, baseboards, chair legs, carpet spotting, floor scrubbing, and detail dusting should be scheduled, not forgotten.
  15. Quality control. Ask how issues are reported, who checks the work, and how quickly missed items are corrected.

What a Good Cleaning Provider Should Ask You

The best walkthroughs are not one-sided. The cleaning company should ask when the building is occupied, what hours work best for cleaning, whether there are sensitive areas, how employees use the breakroom, what customer-facing areas matter most, and what has frustrated you about past service. They should also ask whether you want a simple maintenance clean or a higher-detail presentation standard for clients and visitors.

For Phoenix buildings, they should also ask about dust patterns. Offices near construction, busy roads, desert landscaping, warehouse doors, or heavy parking lot traffic often need a different plan than buildings in low-dust settings. Entryway care, high dusting, vents, and hard floor maintenance may need to be built into the scope from the beginning.

Red Flags During the Walkthrough

Be cautious if a company gives a price without looking at restrooms, flooring, access points, trash flow, and employee break areas. Also be cautious if the scope is described only as “basic cleaning” without listing tasks. Vague language can make it difficult to hold anyone accountable later.

Red FlagWhy It Matters
Quote based only on square footageTwo buildings with the same size can have very different restroom counts, traffic, dust, and floor care needs.
No discussion of frequencySome areas need nightly attention while others may only need weekly or monthly detail work.

No written task listIf the scope is not written down, expectations become hard to verify.

What You Should Receive After the Walkthrough

After the visit, you should receive a written scope that explains what areas are included, how often key tasks are performed, what supplies are included or excluded, and what the monthly or per-visit price covers. The quote should also make clear whether periodic tasks like floor care, interior glass, high dusting, and deep cleaning are included or priced separately.

The goal is not to create a complicated contract. The goal is to remove guesswork before cleaning starts. When the walkthrough is specific, both sides know what “clean” means, and the cleaning crew can follow a realistic plan instead of trying to interpret vague promises.

The Bottom Line

A strong commercial cleaning walkthrough protects your business from under-scoped service and protects the cleaning company from unrealistic assumptions. It should identify the areas that matter most, the conditions that affect the building, and the practical schedule needed to maintain it.

For Phoenix businesses, that means paying close attention to dust, entryways, flooring, restrooms, breakrooms, security, and periodic detail work. If a provider takes time to inspect those items and writes them into the scope, you are much more likely to get consistent cleaning after the contract starts.
